# 2.2 Jungian Depth Psychology

## 2.2.1 The Collective Unconscious and Archetypes

Carl Gustav Jung's concept of the collective unconscious provides a crucial bridge between evolutionary psychology and depth psychology. Jung (1969) argued that beneath the personal unconscious lies a deeper, universal layer shared by all humanity:

> "But this personal unconscious rests upon a deeper layer, which does not derive from personal experience and is not a personal acquisition but is inborn. This deeper layer I call the collective unconscious. I have chosen the term 'collective' because this part of the unconscious is not individual but universal; it has contents and modes of behaviour that are more or less the same everywhere and in all individuals. It is, in other words, identical in all men and thus constitutes a common psychic substrate of a suprapersonal nature which is present in every one of us." (Jung, 1969, pp. 3-4)

This formulation is compatible with contemporary evolutionary psychology in that both recognize universal, inherited psychological structures. The collective unconscious is not a mystical concept but an evolutionary inheritance—patterns accumulated through ancestral experience.

### The Nature of Archetypes

Archetypes are the primary contents of the collective unconscious. Jung conceptualized them as inherited psychological forms, analogous to biological instincts:

> "A primordial image is determined as to its content only when it has become conscious and is therefore filled out with the material of conscious experience. Its form, however... might perhaps be compared to the axial system of a crystal, which, as it were, preforms the crystalline structure in the mother liquid, although it has no material existence of its own... The archetype in itself is empty and purely formal, nothing but a *facultas praeformandi*, a possibility of representation which is given *a priori*. The representations themselves are not inherited, only the forms, and in that respect they correspond in every way to the instincts, which are also determined in form only." (Jung, 1969, p. 80)

This definition is crucial: archetypes are not specific images or ideas but **formal patterns**—templates that structure psychological experience. Just as evolutionary psychology proposes evolved psychological mechanisms (Buss, 2019; Tooby & Cosmides, 1992), Jung proposed evolved psychological forms.

Jung explicitly linked archetypes to evolutionary inheritance:

> "Archetypes reflect the entire psychic structure developed both upwards and downwards by [one's] ancestors in the course of the ages." (Jung, 1969, p. 279)

This establishes archetypes as products of evolutionary history, not cultural constructs.

## 2.2.2 Anima and Animus: The Contra-Sexual Archetypes

Central to understanding mate selection are the anima (the unconscious feminine in men) and animus (the unconscious masculine in women). These archetypes govern attraction, projection, and relationship dynamics.

### Biological Foundations

Jung grounded these archetypes in biology:

> "The anima is projected upon women. Either sex is inhabited by the opposite sex up to a point, for, biologically speaking, it is simply the greater number of masculine genes that tips the scales in favour of masculinity. The smaller number of feminine genes seems to form a feminine character, which usually remains unconscious because of its subordinate position." (Jung, 1969, p. 57)

Here Jung anticipated contemporary understanding of sexual differentiation at the genetic level. The anima/animus are not metaphorical but reflect the biological reality that each sex carries genetic information for both masculine and feminine development.

### Projection in Mate Selection

The primary mechanism by which anima/animus influence mate selection is **projection**—the unconscious externalization of internal archetypes onto potential partners. Jung observed that this projection creates the experience of falling in love:

> "The anima no longer crosses our path as a goddess, but, it may be, as an intimately personal misadventure, or perhaps as our best venture. When, for instance, a highly esteemed professor in his seventies abandons his family and runs off with a young red-headed actress, we know that the gods have claimed another victim. This is how daemonic power reveals itself to us." (Jung, 1969, pp. 201-202)

This "daemonic power" of projection explains phenomena that evolutionary psychology struggles to account for: why individuals make seemingly irrational mate choices that contradict reproductive fitness, why attraction feels numinous (sacred/overwhelming), and why initial attraction intensity predicts relationship instability.

### Contemporary Relationship Dynamics

Jung directly addressed modern relationship crisis through the lens of anima projection:

> "The American divorce rate has been reached, if not exceeded, in many European countries, which proves that the anima projects herself by preference on the opposite sex, thus giving rise to magically complicated relationships." (Jung, 1969, p. 200)

Written in 1959, this observation remains salient: high divorce rates reflect not merely cultural shifts but the underlying psychological reality that relationships formed through projection inevitably face crisis when projections dissolve.

## 2.2.3 The Shadow and Self

### The Shadow Archetype

The shadow represents everything the ego refuses to acknowledge:

> "The shadow personifies everything that the subject refuses to acknowledge about himself and yet is always thrusting itself upon him directly or indirectly—for instance, inferior traits of character and other incompatible tendencies." (Jung, 1969, p. 285)

In relationships, shadow projection onto partners creates conflict—individuals blame partners for qualities they deny in themselves. Integration of the shadow is thus prerequisite for authentic intimacy.

### The Self and Individuation

The Self represents psychological wholeness—the integration of conscious and unconscious:

> "I use the term 'individuation' to denote the process by which a person becomes a psychological 'in-dividual,' that is, a separate, indivisible unity or 'whole.'... The whole must necessarily include not only consciousness but the illimitable field of unconscious occurrences as well." (Jung, 1969, pp. 275-276)

Individuation—the process of becoming psychologically whole—is necessary for mature relationship formation. Without it, relationships remain dependent on projection rather than authentic connection.

## 2.2.4 Empirical Validation: Archetypes and Infidelity

Recent empirical research validates Jung's theoretical claims. A doctoral dissertation examining 122 adults in committed relationships found that archetypal profiles significantly predict infidelity behaviors:

- **Caregiver and Innocent archetypes** are associated with lower cheating and stronger disapproval of infidelity
- **Jester archetype** is associated with higher cheating and being a victim of infidelity  
- **High life meaning** leads to disapproval of infidelity
- **Five or more committed relationships** correlates with increased likelihood of both cheating and being cheated on

Critically, the research found that:

> "Both archetypes and meaning-in-life variables are significant predictors of infidelity attitudes and behaviors. Gender, race, and age were not significant predictors when archetype and meaning variables were considered."

This finding supports Jung's claim that archetypes represent universal patterns transcending demographics, and validates the integration of Jungian and evolutionary frameworks.

## 2.2.5 Integration with Evolutionary Psychology

Jungian depth psychology complements evolutionary psychology by:

1. **Explaining Individual Variation**: While evolutionary psychology identifies universal patterns, Jungian theory explains why individuals vary in mate preferences and strategies based on archetypal profiles.

2. **Accounting for Projection**: Evolutionary psychology describes mate preference mechanisms; Jung explains the psychological experience of attraction through projection.

3. **Bridging Biology and Psychology**: Archetypes link genetic inheritance (evolutionary history) to psychological experience (subjective attraction).

4. **Providing Developmental Framework**: Individuation offers a pathway for conscious mate selection beyond unconscious projection.

---

## Summary

Jungian depth psychology provides the psychological mechanisms underlying evolutionary mate selection patterns. The collective unconscious and archetypes represent evolved psychological structures—inheritances from ancestral experience that continue to shape contemporary mating dynamics. The anima/animus archetypes govern mate selection through projection, creating both initial attraction and subsequent relationship crisis when projections dissolve. Empirical research validates these theoretical claims, demonstrating that archetypal profiles predict relationship behaviors more accurately than demographic factors. This integration of Jung and evolutionary psychology provides a comprehensive framework for understanding both the universality and individual variation in human mating.
