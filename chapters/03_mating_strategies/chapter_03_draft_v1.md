## Chapter Overview

Chapter 2 established universal mate preferences shaped by parental investment. This chapter examines **how** individuals deploy context-dependent mating strategies.

**Central Question:** How do humans navigate mating decisions across varying contexts (mate availability, resources, own mate value)?

**Strategic Pluralism Theory** (Gangestad & Simpson, 2000): Humans possess a repertoire of mating strategies facultatively deployed based on individual circumstances and environmental conditions.

---

## 3.1 The Fundamental Trade-Off: Short-Term vs. Long-Term Mating

### 3.1.1 Defining Strategies

**Short-Term Mating:**
- Duration: Hours to months
- Commitment: Minimal to none
- Investment: Low
- Examples: One-night stands, casual dating

**Long-Term Mating:**
- Duration: Years to lifetime
- Commitment: High
- Investment: High
- Examples: Marriage, committed relationships

### 3.1.2 Benefits and Costs

**Men's Short-Term Benefits:**
1. Increased reproductive opportunities (minimal cost per mating)
2. Genetic diversity
3. Reduced investment burden
4. Mate sampling

**Men's Short-Term Costs:**
1. STI risk
2. Reputational damage
3. Paternity uncertainty if partner conceives elsewhere

**Women's Short-Term Benefits:**
1. Genetic benefits ("good genes" from high-quality males)
2. Resource extraction
3. Mate switching opportunities

**Women's Short-Term Costs:**
1. Pregnancy without support (ancestrally catastrophic)
2. Reputational damage
3. STI risk
4. Male abandonment

**Prediction:** Men should be more willing to pursue short-term mating due to lower costs and higher benefits.

## 3.2 Sexual Strategies Theory (Buss & Schmitt, 1993)

### 3.2.1 Core Propositions

1. Humans possess **distinct psychological mechanisms** for short-term and long-term mating
2. Men and women face **different adaptive problems** in each context
3. Context shifts which mechanisms dominate

### 3.2.2 Men's Dual Strategies

**Short-Term (Quantity):**
- Relaxed standards
- Rapid arousal
- Variety seeking
- Low investment
- **Evidence:** Men desire ~18 partners vs. women's ~5 (Buss & Schmitt, 1993)

**Long-Term (Quality):**
- Elevated standards
- Commitment signaling
- Paternity assurance
- Resource provisioning
- **Evidence:** Men report higher standards for long-term partners

### 3.2.3 Women's Dual Strategies

**Short-Term (Good Genes):**
- Heightened selectivity even for casual
- Ovulatory shifts (prefer masculinity when fertile)
- Resource extraction
- **Evidence:** Women maintain high standards for short-term partners

**Long-Term (Investment):**
- Resource evaluation (Buss 1989: financial prospects d = 0.90)
- Commitment assessment
- Emotional bonding
- **Evidence:** Women value dependability, resources for long-term

## 3.3 Strategic Pluralism Theory (Gangestad & Simpson, 2000)

### 3.3.1 Extensions to Sexual Strategies Theory

**Key Additions:**

1. **Individual Differences:** Own mate value, life history, attachment style influence strategy
2. **Environmental Calibration:** Sex ratio, pathogen prevalence, resources shape deployment
3. **Continuous Strategies:** Not binary short/long-term but continuum

### 3.3.2 Sociosexual Orientation

**Sociosexuality:** Individual differences in willingness to engage in uncommitted sex

**Unrestricted:** Comfortable with casual sex, many partners
**Restricted:** Requires emotional connection before sex

**Sex Differences:** Men higher SOI on average (d ≈ 0.6)

**Within-Sex Variation:**
- High SOI men: High mate value, pursue quantity
- Low SOI men: Low mate value or high-investing types
- High SOI women: High mate value or seeking genes
- Low SOI women: Lower mate value or prioritizing investment

## 3.4 Sex-Specific Strategic Trade-Offs

### 3.4.1 Men's Quantity-Quality Dilemma

**Quantity Strategy:**
- Many partners, minimal investment
- **Benefit:** More offspring
- **Cost:** Lower survival (no paternal care)

**Quality Strategy:**
- Few partners, high investment
- **Benefit:** Higher offspring survival
- **Cost:** Fewer offspring

**Optimal Depends On:**
- Female willingness (sex ratio)
- Resource abundance (need for paternal care)
- Own mate value
- Paternity confidence

### 3.4.2 Women's Investment-Genes Dilemma

**Good Genes Strategy:**
- Mate with genetically superior males
- **Benefit:** High-quality offspring
- **Cost:** Risk of abandonment

**Good Investment Strategy:**
- Mate with resource-providing males
- **Benefit:** Paternal provisioning
- **Cost:** Potentially lower genetic quality

**Optimal Depends On:**
- Own resources (financial independence)
- Kin support
- Own mate value
- Cultural norms

## 3.5 Contextual Triggers for Strategy Shifts

### 3.5.1 Sex Ratio Effects

**Male Responses:**
- Female surplus → short-term strategy (abundant opportunities)
- Male surplus → long-term strategy (commitment required)

**Female Responses:**
- Female surplus → raise investment standards (Stone et al. r = -0.35)
- Male surplus → can leverage scarcity

### 3.5.2 Resource Availability

**Resource-Scarce:**
- Biparental care essential
- Long-term strategies dominate

**Resource-Abundant:**
- Paternal investment less critical
- Short-term strategies viable

### 3.5.3 Own Mate Value

**High Mate Value:**
- Can pursue either strategy successfully
- Less trade-off required

**Low Mate Value:**
- Limited options
- Must trade off dimensions

## 3.6 Attachment Styles as Developmental Calibration

### 3.6.1 Attachment Theory Basics

**Secure:** Consistent caregiving → comfortable with intimacy
**Anxious:** Inconsistent caregiving → fear of abandonment
**Avoidant:** Rejecting caregiving → discomfort with intimacy

### 3.6.2 Attachment and Mating Strategies

**Secure → Long-Term Oriented**
- Comfortable with commitment
- Low promiscuity
- High relationship satisfaction

**Avoidant → Short-Term Oriented**
- Discomfort with commitment
- High promiscuity
- Emotional distance

**Anxious → Unstable Long-Term**
- Desperate for commitment
- Jealousy, mate guarding
- Accept poor-quality partners

## 3.7 Contemporary Applications

### 3.7.1 Online Dating Strategic Shifts

**Tinder:**
- Low commitment barriers
- Abundant options illusion
- Reduced accountability

**Results:**
- Men pursue quantity (like ~50% profiles)
- Women maintain selectivity (like ~14%)
- Extreme asymmetry

### 3.7.2 Hookup Culture

**For Men:** Enables quantity strategy
**For Women:** Strategic dilemma (pressure vs. long-term interests)

**Evidence:** Women report more negative emotions, regret after hookups

### 3.7.3 Relationship Formation Failures

**Modern Pattern:**
- Delayed marriage
- Increased singlehood (30%)
- Rising male celibacy (28% of men 18-30)

**Explanation:**
- Male abundance illusion → short-term orientation
- Female hypergamy squeeze → cannot find commitment-worthy males
- Strategic mismatch

---

## 3.8 Synthesis: Strategic Mismatches in Modernity

### 3.8.1 Three Key Mismatches

**1. Male Abundance Illusion (Online):**
- Perception: Abundant options
- Reality: Most men receive few matches
- Result: Pursue short-term despite low success

**2. Female Hypergamy Squeeze:**
- Ancestral: Seek higher-resource males
- Modern: Women out-earn men
- Result: Pool of "acceptable" males shrinks

**3. Contraception Decoupling:**
- Ancestral: Sex → pregnancy → demand commitment
- Modern: Contraception removes risk
- Result: Male commitment incentives reduced

---

## 3.9 Conclusion

Human mating reflects **strategic pluralism**—facultative strategies calibrated to context.

**Key Insights:**
1. Dual strategies (short-term vs. long-term) with sex-specific emphases
2. Contextual calibration (sex ratio, resources, mate value)
3. Attachment styles as developmental calibration
4. Modern mismatches create novel strategic dilemmas

**Contemporary mating crisis = collision of evolved strategies with unprecedented conditions:**
- Online dating illusions
- Female economic independence
- Contraception
- Sex ratio imbalances

Understanding strategic pluralism enables:
- Explaining modern frustrations
- Designing interventions
- Predicting future trends
