### **1. TRIVERS (1972) - Parental Investment and Sexual Selection**

**Relationship:** SST **operationalizes and extends** Trivers's foundational theory

**Integration File:** 
otes/integration_notes/by_source/trivers_1972_integration.md

| Trivers (1972) Abstract Principle | Buss & Schmitt (1993) Testable Prediction |
|-----------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| "The sex that invests more will be choosier" | Women desire 1 partner in 2 years; men desire 8 |
| "The sex that invests less will compete more" | Men accept stranger sex (75%); women (0%) |
| "Parental investment asymmetry → mating psychology" | Men prioritize fertility cues; women prioritize resources |

**Chapter 2 Integration Strategy:**
> "Trivers (1972) provided the theoretical foundation by identifying parental investment asymmetry as the key selection pressure. Buss & Schmitt (1993) transformed this abstract principle into Sexual Strategies Theory, generating specific testable predictions: men should desire more sexual partners (8 vs. 1 over 2 years), lower standards for short-term mates (75% vs. 0% accepting stranger sex), and prioritize fertility cues, while women should prioritize resource acquisition and commitment indicators."

**Key Quote Connection:**
- **Trivers:** "The sex that invests more in offspring will be more selective in mate choice"
- **Buss & Schmitt:** "An ancestral woman having sex with 100 men in 1 year could still not produce more than a single child. Thus, men are predicted to devote a larger share of their mating effort, compared with women, to sexual access to a variety of partners."

### **2. BUSS (1989) - Sex Differences in Human Mate Preferences (37 Cultures)**

**Relationship:** SST **explains the mechanism** behind Buss's empirical findings

**Integration File:** 
otes/integration_notes/by_source/buss_1989_integration.md

| Buss (1989) Finding | SST Explanation |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| Women value financial prospects (d=0.90, 36/37 cultures) | Long-term mating strategy: solve resource acquisition problem |
| Men value physical attractiveness (d=-0.81, 37/37 cultures) | Long-term mating strategy: solve fertility identification problem |
| Women prefer older partners (+3.42 years) | Older men = more accumulated resources |
| Men prefer younger partners (-2.66 years) | Younger women = higher reproductive value |

**Chapter 3 Integration Strategy:**
> "While Buss (1989) documented cross-cultural universals in mate preferences, Buss & Schmitt (1993) explained *why* these universals exist through Sexual Strategies Theory. Women's universal preference for financial prospects (d=0.90) reflects a long-term mating strategy designed to solve the adaptive problem of resource acquisition for offspring. Men's universal preference for youth and physical attractiveness reflects a strategy to identify fertile mates with high reproductive value."

**Key Innovation:**
- **Buss (1989):** "What do people prefer?" (descriptive)
- **Buss & Schmitt (1993):** "Why do they prefer it, and when does it change?" (mechanistic + contextual)

### **3. STONE ET AL. (2007) - Sex Ratio and Mate Preferences**

**Relationship:** Stone et al. **empirically tests SST's contextual predictions**

**Integration File:** 
otes/integration_notes/by_source/stone_shackelford_buss_2007_integration.md

| SST Prediction (1993) | Stone et al. Finding (2007) |
|-----------------------|----------------------------|
| Men should lower standards when mating opportunities abundant | Men: r = -0.18 (p < .001) with sex ratio across 15 attributes |
| Women should raise resource standards when high-value men scarce | Women's financial prospects preference: r = -0.35 (p < .01) |
| Preferences shift based on ecological context | Effects persist controlling for GNP, across 36 cultures |
| Short-term vs. long-term strategy activation | Male-surplus = short-term activation; male-scarcity = long-term amplification |

**Chapter 7 Integration Strategy:**
> "Stone et al. (2007) provided the first large-scale test of Sexual Strategies Theory's contextual predictions. In male-surplus environments (high sex ratio), men activated short-term mating strategies, lowering standards across 15 attributes (r = -0.18). In male-scarcity environments (low sex ratio), women amplified long-term mating priorities, dramatically raising standards for financial prospects (r = -0.35). This represents SST's temporal context effects manifesting as ecological context effects—sex ratio acts as a 'strategy switch' activating different mating psychologies."

**Critical Connection:**
- **SST (1993):** "Preferences shift across temporal contexts" (short vs. long-term)
- **Stone (2007):** "Preferences shift across ecological contexts" (sex ratio)
- **Mechanism:** Same underlying system, different environmental trigger

### **4. GANGESTAD & SIMPSON (2000) - Strategic Pluralism Theory**

**Relationship:** Strategic Pluralism **extends SST** by adding individual differences

**Integration File:** 
otes/integration_notes/by_source/gangestad_simpson_2000_integration.md *(to be created)*

| SST (1993) | Strategic Pluralism (2000) |
|------------|----------------------------|
| Both sexes have short-term + long-term strategies | **Plus:** Individuals differ in strategy mix (sociosexuality) |
| Strategies shift based on temporal context | **Plus:** Strategies calibrated to developmental environment |
| Focus on universal adaptations | **Plus:** Individual differences are adaptive (conditional strategies) |

**Chapter 3 Integration Strategy:**
> "Gangestad & Simpson (2000) built directly on Sexual Strategies Theory by adding the dimension of individual differences. While SST focused on universal mechanisms shared by all men and all women, Strategic Pluralism Theory explained why some individuals are more oriented toward short-term mating (unrestricted sociosexuality) while others favor long-term (restricted). This reflects facultative calibration to childhood environments: harsh, unpredictable environments → short-term orientation; stable, resource-rich environments → long-term orientation."

**Theoretical Evolution:**
1. **Trivers (1972):** Sex differences exist
2. **Buss & Schmitt (1993):** Both sexes have multiple strategies
3. **Gangestad & Simpson (2000):** Individuals differ in strategy deployment
