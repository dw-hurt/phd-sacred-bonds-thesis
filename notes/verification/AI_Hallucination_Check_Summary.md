## ❌ CRITICAL ERROR #1: Stone et al. (2007) Mischaracterization

### What Chapter 7 Claims
**Section 7.3**: "Stone, Shackelford, and Buss (2007) conducted the most comprehensive analysis of OSR effects on human mating behavior to date: a **50-state U.S. study** examining relationships between state-level operational sex ratios and six key mating-relevant outcomes..."

**Claimed Statistics** (NOT in Stone et al. 2007):
1. +15% higher marriage rates in male-biased OSR states (β = 0.32, p < .001)
2. -20% lower STI rates (gonorrhea: β = -0.41, p < .001)
3. -12% lower illegitimacy rates (β = -0.28, p < .01)
4. -15% lower teen pregnancy rates (β = -0.31, p < .01)
5. Lower divorce rates (β = -0.19, p < .05)
6. Younger age at first marriage for women (β = -0.24, p < .05)
7. Alaska vs. Mississippi state-level comparisons

### What Stone et al. (2007) Actually Is

**Correct Description**:
- **Design**: Cross-cultural study of **36 countries** (NOT 50 U.S. states)
- **Sample**: 9,809 participants from Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Bulgaria, Canada, China, Colombia, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, UK, Greece, Hawaii, India, Indonesia, Iran, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, New Zealand, Nigeria, Norway, Poland, South Africa, Spain, Sweden, Taiwan, USA, Venezuela, Yugoslavia, Zambia
- **Measures**: 18 mate **PREFERENCE** characteristics (psychological ratings), NOT behavioral outcomes
- **No marriage rates, STI rates, illegitimacy rates, or U.S. state-level data**

**Actual Key Findings**:
1. Men's preferences: 16 of 18 correlated negatively with sex ratio (sign test p < .001), but only 1 significantly (sociability: r = -0.43)
2. Women's preferences: 15 of 18 correlated negatively with sex ratio (p < .01), with 3-6 significant correlations
3. Women RAISE standards in lower sex ratio societies (to avoid deception by short-term seeking males)
4. Effects independent of socioeconomic development

## 💡 PREVENTION STRATEGIES FOR FUTURE WORK

### Lessons Learned
1. **Always verify cross-cultural vs. U.S. studies** - Don't assume study design without checking
2. **Distinguish preferences from behaviors** - Stone et al. measured psychological ratings, not outcomes
3. **Check for conflation of sources** - The claimed statistics may come from Pedersen/South & Trent, not Stone et al.
4. **Verify percentages and effect sizes** - Don't trust AI-generated statistics without source confirmation

### Best Practices Going Forward
1. ✅ For each major citation, pull the actual paper and verify claims
2. ✅ Maintain a "source verification log" with URLs to original papers
3. ✅ Flag any statistics with "VERIFY" until confirmed in original source
4. ✅ When AI provides statistics, ask "What page/table is this from?"
5. ✅ Create reading notes file for each source with direct quotes
