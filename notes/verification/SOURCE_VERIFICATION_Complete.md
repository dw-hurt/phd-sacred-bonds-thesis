#### 7.3.1 Historical U.S. Trends: Pedersen (1991)

Pedersen (1991) conducted a comprehensive analysis of American operational sex ratio trends spanning approximately a century (1890-1990). By examining historical demographic shifts and corresponding changes in family behavior, Pedersen documented systematic relationships between sex ratio imbalances and relationship outcomes at the societal level.

**Key Findings**:

When operational sex ratios favored women (male-biased, more men than women):
- **Lower divorce rates**: Men facing competition from abundant male rivals invested more heavily in maintaining relationships
- **Greater male investment in childcare**: Scarce females could demand higher paternal involvement
- **More stable marriages**: Women possessed greater bargaining power to enforce commitment norms

When operational sex ratios favored men (female-biased, more women than men):
- **Higher divorce rates**: Men facing less competition showed reduced relationship investment
- **Reduced male commitment**: Abundant female alternatives allowed men to pursue short-term strategies
- **Female adaptation**: Women adjusted mating strategies to compete for scarce males

**Interpretation**: Pedersen's historical analysis demonstrates that sex ratio effects operate through shifting bargaining power dynamics. The scarcer sex gains leverage to demand favorable relationship terms, while the more numerous sex must adjust behavioral strategies to successfully attract and retain partners.

#### 7.3.2 Contemporary State-Level Evidence: Kim (2013)

Kim (2013) provided more recent evidence by analyzing operational sex ratios and divorce outcomes across 44 U.S. states using 2000 Census data. Kim operationalized OSR as the ratio of never-married males (age 15+) to never-married females (age 15+), correlating these ratios with statewide divorce rates from the Division of Vital Statistics (2002).

**Key Finding**: State-level operational sex ratio correlated significantly with divorce rate (r = +.40, p < .008).

**Interpretation**: Male-biased states (more unmarried men than women) exhibited HIGHER divorce rates. This initially appears to contradict Pedersen's (1991) finding that male-biased OSRs reduce divorce. However, Kim's finding may reflect women's greater power to INITIATE divorce when dissatisfied in male-biased environments, where abundant male alternatives make leaving relationships less costly.

**State-Level Examples**:

**Male-biased OSR states**:
- Alaska (OSR ≈ 1.15:1): Ranked #1 nationally for divorce rate
- Nevada (OSR ≈ 1.12:1): Ranked #13 nationally for divorce rate

**Female-biased OSR states**:
- Massachusetts (OSR ≈ 0.94:1): Ranked #46 nationally (6th lowest divorce rate)
- Rhode Island (OSR ≈ 0.95:1): Ranked #39 nationally for divorce rate

**Reconciling Pedersen and Kim**: The apparent contradiction may stem from different analytical approaches:
1. **Temporal vs. cross-sectional**: Pedersen examined historical trends over time; Kim examined cross-state variation at one time point
2. **Different OSR measures**: Pedersen analyzed total population ratios; Kim focused on unmarried individuals only
3. **Divorce initiation**: Male-biased OSRs may reduce male-initiated divorces (Pedersen) but increase female-initiated divorces (Kim), with the net effect depending on who typically initiates

#### 7.3.3 Cross-National Behavioral Evidence

**South & Trent (1988)** analyzed 117 countries and found that female-biased operational sex ratios (fewer men than women) were associated with:
- **Higher rates of illegitimate births**: When men are scarce, women face greater difficulty securing commitment before childbearing
- **Relaxed female mate standards**: Women in female-biased societies showed lower selectivity in partner choice

**Barber (2000, 2001)** examined 85 countries and found:
- **Higher teen pregnancy rates** in female-biased sex ratio societies
- **Earlier sexual onset** among women facing male scarcity
- **Shorter women's skirts** in low OSR societies (Barber 1999), interpreted as intensified female-female competition

#### 7.3.4 Synthesis: OSR Effects on Relationship Dynamics

Across historical U.S. trends (Pedersen 1991), contemporary state-level data (Kim 2013), and cross-national analyses (South & Trent 1988; Barber 2000, 2001), a consistent pattern emerges:

**Male-biased OSR (more men than women)**:
- Males must compete intensively → increased male investment
- Females possess bargaining power → can demand commitment
- Ambiguous effects on divorce: Lower male-initiated divorce, but potentially higher female-initiated divorce
- Shift toward long-term mating strategies

**Female-biased OSR (more women than men)**:
- Females must compete intensively → relaxed female standards
- Males possess bargaining power → reduced commitment
- Higher illegitimacy, teen pregnancy, earlier sexual onset
- Shift toward short-term mating strategies

These findings align with parental investment theory (Trivers 1972) and sex ratio theory (Guttentag & Secord 1983): the scarcer sex can enforce its preferred mating strategy, while the more numerous sex must adapt to secure any mating opportunities.
