## 7.3 Empirical Evidence: Stone et al. (2007) Cross-Cultural Analysis

### 7.3.1 Study Design and Methodology

Stone, Shackelford, and Buss (2007) conducted a landmark **cross-cultural investigation** of sex ratio effects on human **mate preferences** across 36 cultures (N = 9,809 participants: 4,499 men, 5,310 women). Participants from six continents rated the importance of 18 mate preference characteristics (e.g., good financial prospect, emotional stability, chastity, sociability) on a 4-point scale.

Sex ratio data were obtained from United Nations demographic statistics (ages 15-49, circa 1985) for each culture. The sample included countries with diverse sex ratios:
- **Male-biased**: China (1.083:1), India (1.083:1), Taiwan (1.086:1)
- **Balanced**: USA (1.006:1), UK (1.022:1), Greece (1.001:1)  
- **Female-biased**: Brazil (0.993:1), Estonia (0.995:1), Venezuela (0.985:1)

Stone et al. tested two competing hypotheses about how sex ratio affects psychological mate preferences (not behavioral outcomes):

**Classical Hypothesis**: The more numerous sex lowers preference standards to compete for scarce mates.

**Alternative Hypothesis**: In low sex ratio societies (female-biased), men lower standards to pursue short-term mating, while women RAISE standards to avoid deception by uncommitted males.

### 7.3.2 Core Findings: Mate Preference Shifts

**For Men** (Classical Hypothesis Supported):
- 16 of 18 mate preferences correlated negatively with sex ratio (p < .001, binomial test)
- Interpretation: When men are more numerous (lower OSR), they lower their mate preference standards; when scarce (higher OSR), they raise standards
- **Significant finding**: Preference for sociability strongly correlated with sex ratio (r = -0.43, p < .05)

**For Women** (Alternative Hypothesis Supported):
- 15 of 18 mate preferences correlated negatively with sex ratio (p < .01, binomial test)
- **Significant preferences that INCREASE in lower OSR societies**:
  - Mutual attraction/love (r = -0.43, p < .01)
  - Similar educational background (r = -0.38, p < .05)
  - Emotional stability/maturity (r = -0.36, p < .05)
  - Refinement/neatness (r = -0.31, p < .10)
  - Good financial prospect (r = -0.31, p < .10)
- **Exception**: Chastity (no previous sex) correlated positively with sex ratio (r = +0.34, p < .05)
  - Women in male-biased societies value chastity MORE (possibly because fewer chaste men available in female-biased societies where promiscuity is higher)

**Interpretation**: Women respond to male-biased OSR by RAISING standards for long-term partners—not lowering them as Classical Hypothesis predicted. Stone et al. argue this reflects an adaptive mechanism to avoid deception by short-term seeking males who are more common in low OSR (female-biased) environments.

### 7.3.3 Cross-Cultural Patterns

Stone et al.'s findings align with behavioral evidence from other cross-cultural studies they cite:
- Lower sex ratio societies (female-biased) show higher promiscuity rates (Schmitt, 2005)
- Higher sex ratio societies (male-biased) show lower divorce rates (Pedersen, 1991)
- Lower sex ratio societies show higher illegitimate birth rates (South & Trent, 1988)

The psychological preference shifts Stone et al. documented provide a mechanism for these behavioral patterns: when men are scarce, women raise their standards but may be forced to accept uncommitted relationships, leading to higher promiscuity and illegitimacy.

### 7.3.4 Effect Sizes and Limitations

**Effect Sizes**: Most correlations were modest (r = -0.30 to -0.45), explaining 9-20% of variance. Only a handful reached statistical significance, suggesting:
1. Sex ratio is ONE of many factors affecting mate preferences (alongside economic development, pathogen prevalence, cultural norms)
2. Sample of 36 countries may be insufficient to detect small effects
3. Preference shifts may be subtle compared to behavioral shifts

**Key Limitation**: Stone et al. measured **long-term** mate preferences, not short-term preferences. Their Alternative Hypothesis for men (that men lower standards for casual sex in low OSR) could not be tested with their long-term preference data. Future research should examine short-term mate preferences separately.

**Methodological Strength**: Controlling for socioeconomic development (GNP, infant survival, life expectancy, urbanization) did not change results, suggesting sex ratio effects are independent of economic factors.

## 7.4 Behavioral Evidence: Synthesizing Related Studies

While Stone et al. (2007) focused on mate **preferences**, numerous studies have documented sex ratio effects on actual **behaviors**:

### 7.4.1 Pedersen (1991): Sex Ratio Theory

Pedersen's theoretical review argued that sex ratio shifts produce behavioral changes across species, including humans. He cited evidence that:
- Male-biased OSR societies have lower divorce rates and more stable marriages
- Female-biased OSR societies show increased male promiscuity

### 7.4.2 South & Trent (1988): Cross-National Analysis

South and Trent analyzed demographic data across multiple countries, finding:
- Lower sex ratio societies have higher rates of illegitimate births
- Earlier age at first marriage in male-biased societies

### 7.4.3 Schmitt (2005): 48-Nation Sociosexuality Study

Schmitt found national sex ratio correlated **r = -0.45 (p < .001)** with mean sociosexuality (see Section 7.4 for full treatment of Schmitt's findings).
