# Larsen (2023) - Key Quotes Database
**Polygyny as Solution to Demographic Collapse**

**Source:** Larsen, H. (2023). An evolutionary case for polygyny to counter demographic collapse. *Frontiers in Psychology, 14*, Article 1285554.

---

## Quote Organization by Dissertation Chapter

### Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations - Evolutionary Psychology & Sexual Selection

**Quote 1: Sex Ratio Theory and Mating Strategy**
> "Sex ratio theory predicts that a perceived lack of men will lead women to engage in more short-term relationships and become more promiscuous, while simultaneously becoming more selective in their standards for long-term partners."

- **Context:** Larsen discusses the paradoxical effects of female-biased perceived sex ratios (PSR), where women simultaneously become more selective and more promiscuous
- **Significance:** Validates Strategic Pluralism Theory (Gangestad & Simpson, 2000) by showing how environmental cues (sex ratio) activate dual mating strategies
- **Chapter Relevance:** Demonstrates evolutionary psychological mechanisms underlying mate choice in prosperity contexts

**Quote 2: Evoked vs. Transmitted Culture**
> "Evoked culture refers to evolved psychological mechanisms universal to all people, which are activated when individuals are exposed to specific environmental conditions...Transmitted culture, by contrast, consists of culturally specific ideas, values, beliefs, and attitudes."

- **Context:** Drawing on Cosmides & Tooby (1992), Larsen distinguishes between innate psychological mechanisms and learned cultural norms
- **Significance:** Explains the mismatch between monogamous cultural norms (transmitted) and polygynous mating psychology (evoked)
- **Chapter Relevance:** Theoretical foundation for understanding culture-biology mismatch in modern mating markets

**Quote 3: Polygynous Mindset Activation**
> "Greater prosperity and equality between the genders appear to increase women's sexual selectivity...this creates a 'polygynous mindset' where women essentially compete over a smaller pool of attractive men."

- **Context:** Explains how economic development paradoxically reduces pair-bonding by activating ancestral polygynous psychology
- **Significance:** Connects economic prosperity to changes in mating strategy, validating hypergamy hypothesis
- **Chapter Relevance:** Links evolutionary theory to contemporary demographic trends

### Chapter 3: Mate Preferences - Universal Patterns and Cultural Variation

**Quote 4: Female Preference Hierarchy**
> "Women's preferences for men of high status remain relatively constant across cultures and historical periods, reflecting evolved mating preferences shaped by sexual selection."

- **Context:** Larsen reviews cross-cultural evidence showing persistent female preferences for status markers
- **Significance:** Aligns with Buss (1989) universal preferences, shows stability despite cultural variation
- **Chapter Relevance:** Core evidence for evolved mate preference mechanisms

**Quote 5: Male Stratification and Reproductive Success**
> "In Norway, 90% of high-salary men are in stable partnerships by age 40, compared to only 40% of low-salary men. Among men born in 1985, 23% had not reproduced by age 45, compared to 14% in previous generations."

- **Context:** Empirical data from Norwegian registries showing relationship between male economic status and reproductive success
- **Significance:** Quantifies male stratification in modern egalitarian society, demonstrates Winner-Take-All dynamics
- **Chapter Relevance:** Empirical validation of sexual selection theory predictions

**Quote 6: Involuntary Childlessness Gender Gap**
> "Involuntary childlessness affects three times as many men as women, with low-skilled men experiencing the most severe exclusion from reproduction."

- **Context:** Data showing asymmetric reproductive outcomes between genders in modern Scandinavia
- **Significance:** Demonstrates sexual selection still operates despite contraception and gender equality
- **Chapter Relevance:** Evidence for intrasexual competition effects on reproductive outcomes

### Chapter 4: Mating Strategies in High-Prosperity Environments

**Quote 7: Temporal Polygyny Mechanism**
> "High-status men may engage in temporal polygyny, having multiple sequential or simultaneous relationships with different women over time, even in nominally monogamous societies."

- **Context:** Explains how de facto polygyny operates in monogamous legal frameworks through serial relationships
- **Significance:** Shows how women "recycle" high-value males, creating effective polygyny without legal recognition
- **Chapter Relevance:** Key mechanism explaining mating market dynamics in prosperity

**Quote 8: U.S. Male Sexual Inactivity Trends**
> "The percentage of American young men reporting no sexual activity in the past year increased from 19% to 31% over two decades, with male virginity among those under 30 rising from 8% to 27% between 2008 and 2018."

- **Context:** Empirical data from General Social Survey showing dramatic increase in male sexual inactivity
- **Significance:** Demonstrates Winner-Take-All dynamics extending to sexual access, not just reproduction
- **Chapter Relevance:** Evidence for strategic pluralism and short-term mating concentration

**Quote 9: Female Selectivity in Prosperity**
> "As prosperity increases, women's economic independence allows them to prioritize mate quality over mate access, leading to higher selectivity and increased singlehood."

- **Context:** Explains mechanism linking economic development to demographic decline
- **Significance:** Validates Bertrand et al. (2021) findings on gender gap and marriage rates
- **Chapter Relevance:** Central to understanding prosperity paradox in mating markets

### Chapter 5: Economic Resources and Mate Value

**Quote 10: Danish Low-Skilled Male Isolation**
> "In Denmark, 45% of men without higher education live alone, compared to much lower rates for educated men and for women across all education levels."

- **Context:** Data showing severe concentration of singlehood among economically disadvantaged males
- **Significance:** Demonstrates that hypergamy persists even in most gender-equal societies
- **Chapter Relevance:** Evidence for economic resources as key male mate value component

**Quote 11: Prosperity Paradox**
> "The very prosperity and gender equality that liberate women from economic dependence on men simultaneously activate evolved preferences for high-status partners, reducing willingness to accept less desirable males."

- **Context:** Core thesis explaining demographic collapse in developed nations
- **Significance:** Resolves paradox of declining fertility accompanying gender equality
- **Chapter Relevance:** Integrates economic and evolutionary explanations for mating patterns

### Chapter 6: Physical Attractiveness and Good Genes Indicators

**Quote 12: Attractiveness Stratification**
> "The concentration of female sexual interest on a minority of highly attractive males mirrors the economic stratification among males, creating parallel hierarchies of genetic and economic fitness."

- **Context:** Larsen discusses how physical attractiveness operates alongside economic status in male stratification
- **Significance:** Shows good genes and good resources operate synergistically in modern markets
- **Chapter Relevance:** Evidence for multiple fitness indicator integration

### Chapter 7: Intelligence, Creativity, and Cognitive Fitness

**Quote 13: Educational Homogamy**
> "Assortative mating by education has increased significantly in recent decades, with highly educated individuals increasingly partnering with similarly educated mates, reducing mating opportunities for less educated individuals."

- **Context:** Trend data showing increasing educational sorting in mate selection
- **Significance:** Demonstrates cognitive fitness indicators becoming more important in prosperity
- **Chapter Relevance:** Validates Miller (2000) fitness indicator theory

### Chapter 8: Anima/Animus and Cultural Archetypes in Mate Selection

**Quote 14: Idealized Masculine Archetype**
> "Modern women's access to economic resources allows them to hold out for partners who more closely match idealized masculine archetypes, reducing willingness to compromise on partner quality."

- **Context:** Discussion of how economic independence enables stricter adherence to mate ideals
- **Significance:** Aligns with Jungian animus concept - women less willing to settle below archetypal ideal
- **Chapter Relevance:** Psychological mechanism underlying increased female selectivity

**Quote 15: Scarcity Perception and Standards**
> "The perception of a scarcity of suitable men, even when absolute numbers are balanced, leads women to raise their standards and delay or forgo partnership rather than accept less desirable alternatives."

- **Context:** Explanation of PSR effects on mate choice psychology
- **Significance:** Shows how subjective scarcity (quality, not quantity) drives behavior
- **Chapter Relevance:** Connects objective demographics to subjective psychological experience

### Chapter 9: Mating Market Dynamics and Demographic Outcomes

**Quote 16: Scandinavian Singlehood Crisis**
> "Scandinavia has the world's highest rates of one-person households at 43-46%, representing a dramatic shift from historical pair-bonding patterns in just a few decades."

- **Context:** Empirical data on household composition in most gender-equal societies
- **Significance:** Shows magnitude of demographic shift in peak prosperity conditions
- **Chapter Relevance:** Primary evidence for prosperity-singlehood relationship

**Quote 17: Norwegian Fertility Collapse**
> "Norwegian fertility rates declined from 1.96 to 1.48 children per woman in just one decade, far below the replacement rate of 2.1."

- **Context:** Rapid fertility decline in wealthy, gender-equal society
- **Significance:** Demonstrates urgency of demographic crisis
- **Chapter Relevance:** Outcome measure for mating market dysfunction

**Quote 18: Finnish Fertility Trajectory**
> "Finland experienced a parallel decline from 1.9 to 1.4 children per woman, with similar patterns across all Nordic countries."

- **Context:** Confirms Norwegian trends are regional pattern, not local anomaly
- **Significance:** Shows systematic relationship between gender equality/prosperity and fertility
- **Chapter Relevance:** Cross-cultural validation of demographic trends

### Chapter 10: Policy Implications and Future Directions

**Quote 19: Polygyny Policy Proposal**
> "Legalizing polygyny could address demographic collapse by allowing high-status men to formally partner with multiple women, reducing female singlehood and potentially increasing fertility through multi-parent household structures."

- **Context:** Larsen's controversial policy recommendation based on evolutionary analysis
- **Significance:** Proposes aligning legal structures with evoked psychology rather than fighting biology
- **Chapter Relevance:** Applied implications of evolutionary mating theory

**Quote 20: Alternative Policy Failures**
> "Traditional pro-natalist policies (financial incentives, childcare subsidies) and mass immigration have failed to reverse fertility decline in developed nations, suggesting deeper psychological mechanisms are at play."

- **Context:** Reviews ineffectiveness of conventional demographic interventions
- **Significance:** Argues that policies ignoring evolutionary psychology are doomed to fail
- **Chapter Relevance:** Justification for evolutionary-informed policy approaches

**Quote 21: Polygyny Risks and Trade-offs**
> "Formal polygyny would intensify male intrasexual competition, potentially increasing violence and social instability, while creating patriarchal structures that could undermine women's autonomy."

- **Context:** Larsen acknowledges serious costs of proposed polygyny legalization
- **Significance:** Shows balanced analysis weighing evolutionary logic against social costs
- **Chapter Relevance:** Critical evaluation of policy implications

**Quote 22: Multi-Parent Household Benefits**
> "Polygynous households could provide children with access to multiple caregivers and pooled economic resources, potentially improving developmental outcomes compared to single-parent households."

- **Context:** Potential benefits of formalized multi-partner family structures
- **Significance:** Challenges monogamous nuclear family as only optimal structure
- **Chapter Relevance:** Alternative family structure models in evolutionary context

### Integration Quotes - Connections to Other Sources

**Quote 23: Strategic Pluralism Validation**
> "The data confirm that environmental conditions (prosperity, gender equality) systematically predict shifts in mating strategy, validating theoretical models of strategic pluralism in human mating."

- **Context:** Larsen explicitly connects findings to Strategic Pluralism Theory
- **Significance:** Direct validation of Gangestad & Simpson (2000) framework
- **Chapter Relevance:** Theory integration across Chapter 2 and Chapter 4

**Quote 24: Universal Preferences in Modern Context**
> "Cross-cultural research demonstrates that women's preferences for status, resources, and dominance in male partners remain robust across all levels of gender equality and economic development."

- **Context:** Discussion of preference stability despite cultural change
- **Significance:** Validates Buss (1989, 2003, 2016) universal preference findings
- **Chapter Relevance:** Foundation for Chapter 3 mate preference analysis

**Quote 25: Literature and Mating Archetypes**
> "Romantic literature and media reflect and reinforce female preferences for high-status, dominant males, creating cultural feedback loops that amplify evolved mating psychology."

- **Context:** Analysis of cultural representations of ideal masculinity
- **Significance:** Connects to Jung's collective unconscious and Miller's fitness display theory
- **Chapter Relevance:** Cultural transmission mechanisms in Chapter 8
